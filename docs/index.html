Introduction

A machine learning project using the UCI Chronic Kidney Disease dataset to predict CKD from clinical and lab features.

In this project, we focus on developing a prediction model for chronic kidney disease (CKD) using a dataset obtained from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The dataset was collected from hospital records over a period of approximately two months and is designed to aid in predicting the occurrence of CKD.

The dataset encompasses a variety of attributes related to patient health. We explore and preprocess the data, select appropriate features, and apply classification algorithms to achieve accurate predictions. Key steps include comprehensive EDA (exploratory data analysis), data visualization to identify patterns and trends, and feature engineering to enhance the dataset for modeling.

Dataset at a glance

Multivariate, classification task; 400 samples, 24 features.

Dataset characteristics: Type: Multivariate · Subject area: Other · Associated tasks: Classification · Feature type: Real · Number of instances: 400 · Number of features: 24.

Features include age, blood pressure, specific gravity, albumin, sugar, blood glucose random, blood urea, serum creatinine, sodium, potassium, haemoglobin, packed cell volume, white and red blood cell counts, plus categorical flags (red blood cells, pus cell, pus cell clumps, bacteria, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, appetite, pedal edema, anemia) and the binary target: ckd vs not ckd.

Data cleaning and exploration

Cleaning the data so it is suitable for effective analysis.

Data cleaning removes inconsistencies, errors, and inaccuracies that can skew results. Steps taken:

  • First step: replace ambiguous column names with more descriptive ones. Clear and descriptive column names make the dataset easier to understand and work with.
  • The categorical columns were examined for inconsistencies. For example, columns like diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and class contained variations such as extra spaces or tab characters that could lead to incorrect interpretations.
  • The class column, which originally contained categorical labels ('ckd' and 'not ckd' for kidney disease), was mapped to numeric values (0 and 1). This step is important for machine learning models that require numerical input rather than categorical labels.
  • We observed missing values in several columns. To address these, we first needed to understand the missing mechanism (MCAR, MAR, or MNAR). Once understood, we used imputation techniques such as mean/mode imputation or random sampling; these steps are explained in depth later in the analysis.

Correlation heatmap

Relationships between biological factors and implications for CKD.

Positive correlations:

  • Specific gravity → Red blood cell count, packed cell volume, haemoglobin: Specific gravity measures solute concentration in urine. Higher values may indicate dehydration and can relate to increases in RBC, PCV, and haemoglobin. As kidney function changes, the body’s ability to concentrate urine may shift, linking to blood parameters.
  • Sugar → Blood glucose random: Expected positive correlation; blood glucose reflects sugar in the blood. High blood sugar is a key indicator of diabetes, a major risk factor for CKD. Elevated glucose can damage the kidneys over time.
  • Blood urea → Serum creatinine: Both are waste products filtered by the kidneys. Rising levels typically indicate impaired kidney function and are used to diagnose and monitor CKD.
  • Haemoglobin → Red blood cell count, packed cell volume: Haemoglobin carries oxygen in red blood cells; PCV is the proportion of blood that is red cells. These positive correlations reflect blood health and are important for assessing anemia, which is common in CKD due to reduced erythropoietin production.

Negative correlations:

  • Albumin, blood urea → Red blood cell count, packed cell volume, haemoglobin: Albumin can leak into urine when kidneys are damaged. Higher blood urea and negative correlation with RBC/PCV/haemoglobin can indicate worsening kidney function and anemia.
  • Serum creatinine → Sodium: As kidney function worsens (rising creatinine), the kidneys’ ability to maintain sodium balance can be compromised, potentially leading to hyponatremia and serious consequences.

Monitoring PCV is important in assessing the severity of anemia in CKD patients and determining the need for interventions that can improve red blood cell production and oxygen-carrying capacity, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Assessment of missingness

Understanding and handling missing data.

NMAR analysis:

  • White blood cell count and red blood cell count: Possibly the missing data in these features is related to low or abnormal values, which could indicate NMAR (e.g. if severe anemia cases were more likely to have missing data). This is likely not the case, as low RBC and white blood cell count are included in the dataset.
  • Potassium, sodium: Similar reasoning applies if missing values were associated with abnormal or severe health conditions. However, the data does in fact possess low and even extreme potassium and sodium levels, so this does not seem to be the case.
  • Packed cell volume, pus cell, haemoglobin: If missing data were related to critical health conditions or specific thresholds, this could indicate NMAR. Taking a glance at the dataset, this is likely not the case—we see plenty of low haemoglobin levels.

Taking a look at the graphs generated before, these are likely not NMAR.

Testing columns for missingness: Each column in the dataset was tested to determine if the missing data was dependent on other variables. The process involved checking whether the proportion of missing values in a column differed significantly across different groups defined by other columns. The results indicate that the missing data for every column tested seems to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR). This means that the probability of data being missing is not related to the values of any observed or missing data, and the missingness is purely random.

The function test_missingness() compared the proportion of missing values in a specified column across different groups defined by another column. For each column, the proportion of missing values for each group was calculated. A statistical test (ANOVA or t-test) was performed to see if these proportions significantly differed across groups. If the p-value was below a threshold (e.g. 0.05), it suggested that the missingness might be related to the group variable (i.e. not MCAR). The function find_non_mcar_columns() iterated over all columns with missing values and used test_missingness() to test for missingness across all other columns. The final output showed that no significant differences in missingness proportions were found across the groups for any column. Therefore, the missing data in all columns was considered to be MCAR.

Since the missing data is MCAR, the missingness is random and does not depend on any observed or unobserved values. The missing data does not introduce systematic bias into the analysis. Random sampling can be used to handle missing values effectively; using random sampling won't introduce bias.

Preprocessing: We used random-value imputation for numeric columns and mode imputation for categorical ones, then label-encoded categoricals. Feature selection was done with chi-squared (SelectKBest); the top 10 features were used for modeling.

Framing the prediction problem

Classification task with accuracy as the main metric.

The model aims to predict the likelihood of chronic kidney disease (CKD). This is a classification task, as we are predicting whether a patient falls into the CKD or non-CKD category. The metric we will use to evaluate our model's performance is accuracy, as it provides a straightforward interpretation of the proportion of correctly classified instances.

At the time of prediction, we will have access to various features, such as serum creatinine levels, blood urea, haemoglobin, and packed cell volume. These features will be used to predict the presence of CKD, offering valuable insights for early diagnosis and effective disease management.

Model preprocessing

Feature encoding and feature selection.

Feature encoding: In the feature encoding step, categorical variables within the dataset are transformed into numerical values using LabelEncoder from sklearn.preprocessing. This process is essential for preparing the data for machine learning algorithms, which generally require numerical input. Many machine learning models cannot directly process categorical data; converting these categories into numerical values allows the models to interpret and analyze the data effectively.

Feature selection: We performed feature selection to improve the prediction of chronic kidney disease (CKD), using the chi-squared test to rank the importance of each feature in the dataset—focusing on how well they contribute to predicting whether a patient has CKD. By selecting the top-ranked features, the model can be more efficient and accurate, as it uses only the most relevant variables. The selected features are then used to train the classification model, helping to enhance its predictive power while reducing the risk of overfitting.

Models and evaluation

KNN, Random Forest, and XGBoost—performance metrics, confusion matrices, and ROC curves.

We explore three machine learning models for classifying CKD vs non-CKD. Each is evaluated on multiple metrics to determine effectiveness. Below are the confusion matrices and ROC curves for each model.

Simple, effective classification based on the majority class among nearest neighbors.

Performance: Training accuracy 100% (perfect on training data, suggesting possible overfitting). Test accuracy 69% (decent but the drop from 100% indicates overfitting).

Ensemble of decision trees to improve accuracy and control overfitting.

Summary

Random Forest and XGBoost generalize well; KNN overfits.

Serum creatinine, haemoglobin, blood urea, albumin, packed cell volume, and related lab and clinical features drive the predictions and align with the biology of CKD. Random Forest (97% test accuracy) and XGBoost (96%) are suitable as reference models; KNN shows overfitting (100% train, 69% test).
